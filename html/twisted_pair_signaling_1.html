Twisted pair signaling

Ethernet sends bits as small changes of voltage on pairs of copper wires. The animation shows how a transistor connects a wire to a high or a low voltage rail. The notes below explain each part of it, then what noise does to that voltage and how the receiver still reads the right bit.

How a wire gets its voltage

Sending a 0: the bottom transistor is on, so wire A is tied to the low rail (0 V).

Reading the diagram

High rail
A copper path held at 3.3 V. A regulator takes a bigger supply, such as a 5 V or 12 V input, and steps it down. It adds or removes charge as needed, so the rail stays at 3.3 V even when electrons flow into it.
Low rail
A copper path connected to the negative side of the supply. It is the reference point, so its voltage is 0 V by definition, like the floor you measure height from. In a real device it is one large copper area shared by the whole board. It holds so many electrons that a wire taking or giving a few does not change its level, which is why it needs no regulator.
Transistor
A switch controlled by a voltage. The gate is a small plate above the silicon, separated from it by a thin insulator. A voltage on the gate creates an electric field that pulls electrons together under it and forms a channel. Without the gate voltage the channel disappears and the switch is off.
Wire A
It connects to one rail at a time, never both. With the top transistor on, wire A copies the high rail and reads as a 1. With the bottom transistor on, it copies the low rail and reads as a 0. The transistor connects the wire to a rail that already has the voltage; it does not make voltage.
Which way the electrons go
Electrons are negative, so they move toward the more positive side. Sending a 1 pulls electrons out of wire A into the high rail. A wire with fewer electrons than normal is more positive, so its voltage rises. Sending a 0 lets electrons flow in from the low rail, and the voltage falls. Wire A only has to lose a tiny fraction of its electrons to reach 3.3 V.
Why the flow stops
Electrons move because there is a voltage difference. When wire A reaches the voltage of the rail it is connected to, nothing pushes them anymore, and wire A stays at that level until the transistors switch.

What a motor does to a wire

A motor has coils carrying changing current and magnets that spin, so the magnetic field around it keeps changing. A changing field pushes on charges, including the free electrons in the copper of a nearby cable. This is called induction. The push is along the wire, and the electrons cannot leave it.

Pushed toward one end, the electrons bunch up there. That end has slightly more electrons than normal and the other end slightly fewer. As in the animation, an uneven spread of electrons is a voltage. Picture a water pipe closed at both ends. Pushing the water toward one end leaves the total unchanged, but the pressure rises at that end and falls at the other. The receiver is a pressure gauge at the end of the pipe. The wire is also part of a loop with the transmitter and the receiver, so the push drives a tiny current through the receiver's input, and that is what it measures.

Having a lot of electrons in the cable does not protect the signal. Voltage depends on the imbalance, and the real signal is also a tiny imbalance, since wire A only has to lose a small fraction of its electrons to reach 3.3 V. Noise creates the same kind of tiny imbalance, so the receiver cannot tell the two apart. If the push is small compared with the signal, for example a few millivolts against about a volt, it does not matter, which is why most cables work next to appliances. It becomes a problem with a strong motor, a long cable that catches more of the field, or a small signal.

The motor's field keeps changing direction, so the electrons move back and forth at the motor's frequency. The noise wiggles up and down on top of the signal.

How voltage is measured

Voltage always exists between two points. A voltmeter has two probes. The extra electrons at one point repel each other and push toward a point that has fewer, and the meter measures how strong that push is between its probes. It does not count electrons. A single wire has no voltage of its own. A voltage exists between wire A and wire B, or between a wire and ground.

The receiver does the same with its two inputs. One is connected to wire A and the other to wire B, both at the receiver end of the cable. A sensitive amplifier senses the difference in push between them and lets almost no current through, so measuring does not disturb the signal. Voltage is not exactly the imbalance. The imbalance creates the push, and the voltage is how strong that push is between two points.

How a bit is sent and read

The receiver does not pick the wire that has fewer electrons. It computes A − B. If the result is above 0 V it reads a 1, and if it is below 0 V it reads a 0. The transmitter should therefore send two states whose differences have opposite signs and sit as far from zero as possible. The table compares two ways to do it, using example voltages.

The second scheme can work, but its margin is four times smaller.

Opposite voltages have two more benefits. The two wires always carry opposite voltages, so their average is 0 V and the fields they radiate cancel at a distance, which reduces crosstalk into neighboring pairs. The signal also has no steady offset, which suits the transformer in an Ethernet port. The receiver ignores the absolute levels. A = 0 V with B = −1 V gives the same difference as A = +1 V with B = 0 V.

When noise flips a bit

Only the unequal part of the noise can flip a bit. Take a 0 sent as A = −1 V and B = +1 V, so A − B = −2 V, and a motor that adds +3 V.

Equal noise cancels in the subtraction. In the last row the unequal part (3 V) is larger than the signal's margin (2 V), so the sign flips.

When a bit flips, the frame's checksum (the CRC) does not match and the frame is dropped. A higher layer such as TCP sends it again, which shows up as lag, slow transfers, or a link that keeps dropping.

Twisting gives both wires the same push

Noise is stronger on the wire that is closer to its source. In parallel wires the same wire is always the closer one, so the pushes differ. Twisting swaps the two wires every few millimeters, so each spends about half its length near the source and half away from it. The twists also alternate direction, so the push along one twist is partly cancelled by the next.

Both wires end up shifted by almost the same amount. Each wire alone still measures something different from what was sent, but A − B equals what the transmitter sent, and the bit is read correctly. The cancellation is not perfect. A small unequal part is left, far smaller than the signal's margin.
